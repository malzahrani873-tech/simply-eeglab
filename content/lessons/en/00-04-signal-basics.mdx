## The idea

An EEG signal is a mix of waves at different speeds, some slow and some fast. Filtering, resampling and spectral analysis all deal with these speeds.

To understand those steps you need three ideas: **frequency**, **sampling rate** and the **spectrum**. So how do you see the frequencies mixed into one jagged line?

## How does the same signal look in time and in the spectrum?

<FilterDemo filters="off" />

**Frequency** is how many times a wave repeats per second, measured in hertz: 10 Hz means 10 times per second. The **spectrum** is a plot of how strong each frequency is in the signal.

In the **Time** view you see one jagged line that is hard to read. In the **Spectrum** view you see the same signal broken down into its frequencies: a peak at 10 Hz (alpha), a smaller peak at 6 Hz (theta), and a narrow peak at 60 Hz (power line).

Try it yourself:

1. Choose the **Spectrum** view.
2. Find the highest peak between 8 and 12 Hz. This is the alpha peak.
3. Find the very narrow peak at 60 Hz. This is power-line noise, not brain activity.
4. Switch the line frequency to **50 Hz**. Notice that the power-line peak moves, while the alpha peak stays in place.
5. Go back to the **Time** view and press **New signal** a few times. The time shape changes, while the peaks in the spectrum stay in place.

**Simplifications in the simulation:** one channel with an artificial signal, not real EEG, at a fixed sampling rate of 250 Hz. Leave the two filters for now; you will come back to them in the [filtering lesson](/en/lesson/filtering/). You will open the spectrum in EEGLAB itself from <MenuPath>Plot > Channel spectra and maps</MenuPath>, in the [filtering](/en/lesson/filtering/) and [power spectrum](/en/lesson/power-spectrum/) lessons.

## What is the sampling rate, and why can no frequency above half of it appear?

The system does not record a continuous line. It records one point every fraction of a second. At 128 Hz it records 128 samples per second, one sample about every 7.8 milliseconds.

- **Nyquist frequency:** the highest frequency that can be represented is half the sampling rate. At 128 Hz that is 64 Hz.
- **Aliasing:** a wave faster than the Nyquist limit does not disappear; it shows up disguised at a lower frequency. A 70 Hz wave recorded at 128 Hz shows up at 58 Hz (128 minus 70). This is why EEGLAB applies a low-pass filter before lowering the sampling rate (EEGLAB tutorial, Resampling page).

| Dataset | Sampling rate | Nyquist frequency |
|---|---|---|
| `eeglab_data.set` (main path) | 128 Hz | 64 Hz |
| PhysioNet file in station 2 | 160 Hz | 80 Hz |
| A recording at 1000 Hz | 1000 Hz | 500 Hz |

## What do alpha, beta and theta mean?

They are names of common frequency bands, and you will read them in almost every paper.

| Band | Approximate frequency | Where it usually appears |
|---|---|---|
| Delta | Below 4 Hz | Deep sleep |
| Theta | 4 to 8 Hz | Memory and attention tasks, at frontal midline electrodes |
| Alpha | 8 to 12 Hz | Relaxed with eyes closed, at posterior electrodes |
| Beta | 13 to 30 Hz | Over the motor area |
| Gamma | Above 30 Hz | Hard to measure from the scalp because muscle activity covers it |

The band boundaries are a convention among researchers, not a law, and they differ slightly between sources. The alpha peak itself varies from person to person within this range.

## How do I build a signal by hand and read its spectrum?

This script is optional and does not need EEGLAB. It builds a 10-second signal at 128 samples per second (the same rate as `eeglab_data.set`) from parts you already know, then computes its spectrum and looks for alpha in it.

```matlab
% Build a 10-second signal at 128 Hz from known parts, then look at its spectrum
srate = 128;                          % samples per second
t     = (0:10*srate-1) / srate;       % 10 s of time, first sample at 0
rng(0);                               % same random noise on every run
alpha = 20 * sin(2*pi*10*t);          % 10 Hz, 20 microvolts
theta =  8 * sin(2*pi*6*t);           % 6 Hz, 8 microvolts
noise =  5 * randn(size(t));          % broadband noise
sig   = alpha + theta + noise;

% Amplitude spectrum with the FFT
n     = numel(sig);
amp   = abs(fft(sig)) / n * 2;        % amplitude at each frequency
freqs = (0:n-1) * srate / n;          % frequency of each FFT bin in Hz
keep  = freqs <= srate/2;             % keep frequencies up to Nyquist

figure;
subplot(2,1,1); plot(t, sig); xlim([0 2]);
xlabel('Time (s)'); ylabel('Amplitude (\muV)');
subplot(2,1,2); plot(freqs(keep), amp(keep));
xlabel('Frequency (Hz)'); ylabel('Amplitude (\muV)');

% Strongest frequency between 8 and 12 Hz
band = freqs >= 8 & freqs <= 12;
[~, iPeak] = max(amp .* band);
fprintf('Alpha peak at %.1f Hz, amplitude %.1f\n', freqs(iPeak), amp(iPeak));
fprintf('Nyquist frequency: %d Hz\n', srate/2);

% Aliasing: a 70 Hz wave sampled at 128 Hz shows up at 58 Hz
fast = sin(2*pi*70*t);
[~, iAlias] = max(abs(fft(fast)) .* keep);
fprintf('A 70 Hz wave appears at %.1f Hz\n', freqs(iAlias));
```

**What each part does:**

- `sin(2*pi*10*t)` is a wave that repeats 10 times per second, that is 10 Hz. The number in front of it is its amplitude in microvolts.
- `fft` breaks the signal into a sum of waves and gives the amplitude of each frequency. The lower plot has a peak at 6 Hz and a higher one at 10 Hz.
- The plot stops at 64 Hz, because that is the Nyquist limit at 128 samples per second.
- The last three lines record a 70 Hz wave, which shows up at 58 Hz, as explained in the sampling rate section.

## How do I verify that I read the spectrum right?

- In the simulation you found the alpha peak near 10 Hz and the power-line peak at 60 Hz, and only the power-line peak moved when you switched to 50 Hz.
- The script prints `Alpha peak at 10.0 Hz` with an amplitude close to 20.
- The script prints `Nyquist frequency: 64 Hz`, and the lower plot stops at 64 Hz.
- The script prints `A 70 Hz wave appears at 58.0 Hz`, and you can explain why in one sentence.

## Where do students go wrong reading spectra and sampling rates?

- **Thinking the sampling rate is the highest frequency in the data.** The highest frequency is only half of it. Data at 128 Hz contain no information above 64 Hz.
- **Thinking every peak in the spectrum is brain activity.** The narrow peak at 60 Hz (in Saudi Arabia and the US) or 50 Hz (in Europe and most other Arab countries) comes from the power grid. Check the spectrum before you assume the line frequency from the recording country.
- **Reading the spectrum's power axis as linear.** EEGLAB plots spectral power in decibels (dB), a logarithmic scale (`spectopo.m`). A 10 dB difference means 10 times more power, not 10 units more.
- **Treating band boundaries as exact numbers.** "Alpha is 8 to 12" is a rough rule. If a participant's peak is at 7.5 Hz, it may still be their alpha.
- **Lowering the sampling rate by hand by keeping every other sample.** This causes aliasing. Use the EEGLAB function that filters first; you will learn to use it in the [resampling lesson](/en/lesson/resampling/).
